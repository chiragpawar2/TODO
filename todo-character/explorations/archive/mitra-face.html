Mitra

Eyes only, behind one pane of dark glass. No mouth, no face outline, no eyebrows — every reaction is carried by eye shape, lid, gaze and glow. He is readable from across the living room, costs almost nothing to draw on a Raspberry Pi screen, and never has to fake a lip‑sync.

At rest idle Nobody has said anything for a while. He drifts and blinks.

At the size he will actually be

A 2.8‑inch panel on the Pi, roughly 320×240. The eyes hold up because they are two solid shapes — thin lines and small details would disappear here.

Same renderer, no changes. Everything that reads at 460 px still reads at 160 px, which is the whole argument for eyes over a drawn face.

Every state he can be in

These are not invented moods. Each one is a branch that already exists in Brain.ask(), so the face has something true to show at every moment of a turn.

Rules that keep him feeling alive

  • He always returns to rest.No expression holds longer than two seconds except rest and sleep. A face frozen in delight reads as broken.
  • He blinks on a human rhythm.Every 3 to 6 seconds, randomised, with the occasional double blink. Perfectly regular blinking is the single clearest tell that something is a machine.
  • Never two expressions at once.One state owns the face. Transitions take 180 ms, which is fast enough to feel like a reaction and slow enough not to twitch.
  • Glow carries urgency, shape carries feeling.Shape says what he feels; brightness says how much he wants your attention. Keeping them separate stops the face getting shouty.
  • Asymmetry means uncertainty.Both eyes matching reads as confident. One eye smaller, or a tilt, is reserved for not knowing — so it stays meaningful.
  • He looks away when he thinks, at you when he listens.Holding eye contact while processing feels like staring. Breaking it is what makes the thinking state readable without words.

Why “Mitra”

It means friend in Marathi, Hindi and Sanskrit, so it carries the same warmth for everyone in the house. It also happens to be a good wake word for Stage 5: two syllables, a hard consonant to latch onto, and nothing else in a kitchen sounds like it.
