# Give the eyes a mouth that mostly hides

Every home robot that shipped a character face converged on the same answer — two large animated eyes on a small emissive panel, no mouth — and the verified tally is roughly **8 eyes-only products to 1 full-face product**, with the sole full-face product (Embodied Moxie) dead since December 2024. Yet the only controlled, single-variable study of rendered robot faces found the exact opposite: removing the mouth made a face **significantly less friendly, less trustworthy, less likable, less intelligent and more machine-like**, with participants calling it scary and spontaneously assigning it to *surveillance* work ([Kalegina et al. 2018](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf)). A separate 305-participant study put full-face emotion recognition at **64.4%** against **51.3%** for the eye region alone, with happiness degrading most ([arXiv:2410.14337](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14337v1)). This report argues the contradiction is not a real disagreement about faces: it is a disagreement about *animation and physical size*. Kalegina's stimuli were static images and her own authors concede a pupil-less face "may not appear as 'soulless' if it is able to blink"; the shipped products are all animated, most at a physical panel size where a mouth would be a few pixels of noise. No product shutdown in the record was ever attributed to face design, so the eyes-only convention's survival validates nothing. For a Raspberry Pi shelf device in an Indian household, the defensible direction is an **animated eyes-first face with pupils and a minimal single-stroke mouth that is present for happy, sad and neutral and can retract elsewhere** — redundantly encoding every state in both eye shape *and* mouth, because no study places Indian participants on the eyes-versus-mouth axis at all.

## Eight eyes-only products to one full face, and the one full face is dead

The commercial grammar is startlingly uniform. Anki's Vector put two pupil-less cartoon eyes on a **184 × 96** panel whose active area is **23.2 × 12.1 mm** — a postage stamp ([anki_vector/screen.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/anki/vector-python-sdk/master/anki_vector/screen.py)). Cozmo before it used an OLED with eyes that have no pupils and change shape to show mood, animated under ex-Pixar animator **Carlos Baena**, and morph into a game of Pong when bored ([designboom](https://www.designboom.com/technology/anki-cozmo-07-07-2016/); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2016/06/27/anki-pixar-dreamworks-robot-games/)). Sony's aibo uses **two OLED displays** that blink, close and track the owner around the room ([Sony](https://sony.mediaroom.com/2018-08-23-Sony-Announces-Limited-First-Litter-Edition-Release-of-aibo-in-U-S); [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/sony-advanced-aibo-robot-dog-unleashed)). Groove X's LOVOT builds each eye from **six stacked display layers** forming iris and pupil to hold a returned gaze ([Japan Gov Online](https://gov-online.go.jp/eng/publicity/book/hlj/html/202106/202106_10_en.html)).

The strongest statement of the rule is Mayfield's. Kuri's designers said flatly: *"We realized that if Kuri had a screen, and the screen showed anything other than eyes, Kuri would break character and become an object"* ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/mayfield-robotics-announces-kuri-a-700-mobile-home-robot)). Kuri consequently had **no general display at all** — beeps, head motion and eyes. Amazon Astro is the controlled experiment that fails Kuri's rule: eyes that wink and blink on a **10.1in 1280 × 800** panel are credited with making it feel less like surveillance-on-wheels ([Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/news/amazon-astro)), but a reviewer noted the **eyes disappear whenever Alexa responds**, reverting to an Echo screen and breaking the illusion ([review via BusinessGhana](https://businessghana.com/site/news/technology/264104/Amazon-Astro-review-too-much-Alexa-not-enough-arms)). NIO's in-car NOMI arrived at the same prohibition independently and more explicitly: NIO treats the face as a person's face **whose integrity cannot be broken**, and rejected overlaying a fatigue-detection teacup icon on it ([CnEVPost](https://cnevpost.com/2021/03/30/what-is-nomi-heres-everything-you-need-to-know/)).

NOMI is also where the mouthlessness is reasoned rather than assumed. NIO found that adding a mouth made matching voice to mouth movement harder and the mouthless form "more harmonious and reasonable," arguing explicitly from the uncanny valley ([CnEVPost](https://cnevpost.com/2021/03/30/what-is-nomi-heres-everything-you-need-to-know/)). That is a lip-sync argument, not an anti-mouth argument — a distinction the recommendation below turns on.

## The lab says mouth, the market says eyes — and the lab is measuring still photographs

The controlled evidence is unambiguous within its own frame. Kalegina et al. censused **157 rendered robot faces on 76 attributes** and then built 17 synthesised faces differing from a computed "average face" by exactly one feature, rated by **n=50** with Bonferroni-corrected paired t-tests. Three variants came out worst on friendliness, trustworthiness *and* likability simultaneously: **no mouth, no pupils, and eyelids**. No-mouth was the most unfriendly and least likable face of the entire set; across both studies, no-pupil and no-mouth faces were "consistently ranked as unfriendly, machinelike, and unlikable, and were overwhelmingly selected for security-type work," with participants volunteering surveillance framings unprompted and naming the pupil-less face "dead eyes robot" and "soulless robot" ([Kalegina et al. 2018](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf)). The census tells the same story about practice at large: **65.6% of real rendered robot faces have a mouth** and **71.3% have pupils** — the modal rendered robot face is eyes-with-pupils plus a mouth. Shipped home robots are the minority, not the norm, in the wider robot-face population.

So why does the home-robot industry diverge? Four mechanisms, in descending order of strength.

**First, the studies measure static images and the products are animated.** This is not an outside criticism; it is the authors' own stated limitation, and they name the specific consequence: a face without pupils "may not appear as 'soulless' if it is able to blink," and Jibo — rated unfriendly as a still image — "could convey their emotive qualities through motion and thus be less reminiscent of a machine" ([Kalegina et al. §6](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf)). In Study 1 one robot's low friendliness was explicitly offset by "its bodily movements and animated pupil." The eyelid penalty is almost certainly a stimulus artefact in the same way: a drawn, permanently lowered lid reads as a sly squint (participants said "sly" and "smug"), while a lid that exists *only* as a blink is a different design object entirely. The no-mouth face was also shown among sixteen smiling faces, which the authors concede may have exaggerated the reaction. **Every negative static finding is therefore an upper bound on the penalty an animated face would pay, not a measurement of it.**

**Second, motion is empirically worth more than features.** Bennett & Šabanović's MiRAE used only **two linear feature sets** — one upper (eye/brow), one lower (mouth) — with strict adherence to minimal FACS Action Units, cost **$150–175**, and could be built in under a day. Embodied, it scored **Happy 96.7%, Sad 100%, Anger 86.7%, Surprise 96.7%** — matching or beating Kismet, Feelix and BERT, all far more complex ([Bennett & Šabanović, HRI 2013](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf)). Adding **neck motion alone pushed happy, sad, anger and surprise to 100%**. Perceived expression strength correlated with identification accuracy at **r² = .896**. The lesson is that amplitude and motion, not feature count, are the legibility levers — which is precisely the channel a static-image study cannot see and a shipped robot exploits.

**Third, physical size forces the eyes-only choice at the small end.** Vector's face is 23.2 × 12.1 mm at roughly 8 px/mm. At that size a pupil or iris is a handful of pixels and reads as noise, and a mouth stroke competes with the eyes for the only pixels that carry across a room. The signage research explains why: legibility distance is driven overwhelmingly by feature **height**, and "unlike other key variables (e.g., contrast, luminance, and stroke width) legibility distance continues to improve with increases in letter height" ([US Access Board](https://www.access-board.gov/research/communication/variable-message-signing/vms-legibility/)). On a postage stamp, two huge eye shapes are the only thing that survives. **This constraint is yours to choose, not inherit** — a 2.8in panel is not a postage stamp, and at that size the trade-off reverses.

**Fourth, the mouthlessness argument is really a lip-sync argument.** NIO's stated reason was that a mouth made voice-to-mouth matching harder ([CnEVPost](https://cnevpost.com/2021/03/30/what-is-nomi-heres-everything-you-need-to-know/)). That rules out phoneme animation, which a cloud-latency assistant cannot do well anyway; it does not rule out a mouth that changes shape per emotional state and holds. The contrary approach exists in patent form — an eyes-nose-mouth display driven by **phoneme mouth shapes** plus gaze and brow ([US 10,661,433](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10661433)) — and it is exactly the thing to avoid.

What the market record does **not** support is the inference that eyes-only *works*. Across Jibo, Kuri, Anki and Moxie the documented causes are funding, price competition from smart speakers, shipping execution and cloud dependency. **No post-mortem in the record attributes any shutdown to face or character design**; the record is silent, not negative. Face design appears in post-mortems only as the thing reviewers liked — except at Astro, where sharing the panel with Alexa's UI is the one named face-design failure. Survival correlates with recurring revenue (aibo, LOVOT, Romi, RoBoHoN, Gatebox all charge monthly; Jibo, Kuri, Cozmo and Moxie were hardware-margin businesses), which is orthogonal to the face. **Survivorship cannot validate eyes-only, and the eight-to-one tally is a convention, not a result.** No source found researches the convention *as* a convention; Kuri's quote is the only first-hand rationale in existence.

One more asymmetry deserves naming. The faceless platforms — Alexa, Siri, Google Assistant, Bixby — have published essentially **no rationale for rejecting a face**; repeated targeted searching found nothing from Amazon or Apple, and the likeliest reading is that facelessness was inherited from the product category (a speaker, an OS layer) rather than deliberated. Microsoft's Marcus Ash said only that the team "settled pretty quickly on the idea of a simple geometric shape" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2015/02/11/cortana_details_windows_10/)). The one crisp rejection on evidence is Mercedes': a human face was prototyped, user-tested and killed because "quite a lot more people dislike the human face," replaced by a star cloud that "represents something like a human being, but it's not a face" ([InsideEVs](https://insideevs.com/news/704386/mercedes-ai-human-face/)) — though the study itself is unpublished, so only Schäfer's characterisation is available. Crucially, Mercedes rejected a **photoreal human** face while NIO's **cartoon-abstract mouthless** face won a Red Dot and tested well. Those two results bracket the uncanny valley from either side rather than contradicting each other. And the faceless camp supplies the strongest argument against itself: Cortana's ring expressed **18 different moods**, many of which "looked nearly identical" ([PCWorld](https://www.pcworld.com/article/431767/cortanas-ui-now-expresses-18-different-emotions-siri-remains-detached-and-aloof.html)). An abstract primitive hits a discrimination ceiling somewhere around six to eight states. That is journalistic observation, not a measured discrimination study, but it is the only evidence on the question.

## The concrete feature rules, each with its finding attached

Every rule below is tied to a measured effect. Where the effect is unmeasured, the table says so.

| Decision | Rule | Evidence behind it |
|---|---|---|
| **Overall realism** | Moderately detailed, clearly non-realistic. Keep stylisation level internally consistent — never one photoreal element in a flat geometric face | The friendliest-rated real faces (Yumi, FURo-D, Buddy, Datou) "had relatively detailed faces, but were not exceedingly realistic." Participants preferred **less realistic and less detailed** robots specifically for the **home** context. Perceptual *mismatch*, not realism, is the best-supported route to eeriness ([Kalegina](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf); [Kätsyri et al. 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4392592/)) |
| **Having a face at all** | Yes. Even a bad face beats none | Broadbent et al.: an "uncanny" silver face display promoted perceptions of agency relative to a *no-face* display (cited in [Kalegina §2](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf)) |
| **Feature count** | Three to four features. Eyes + pupils + mouth is three; **irises** are the highest-value fourth | Four-or-more features and more complex eyes raise *humanness* (DiSalvo 2002, as cited by Kalegina §5.3.4) — but humanness is not the goal for a home device. The iris variant was the **most-liked face overall** and significantly more human-like, with no maturity or gender side effects |
| **Eye size** | Large. Occupy a substantial fraction of the panel | The "small eyes" variant was **significantly less likable**. 51.6% of real faces have eyes occupying between 1/20 and 1/2 of screen area — descriptive practice, not an optimum. **No study gives an optimal eye-diameter-to-face-width ratio**; Kalegina's large/small are categorical |
| **Eye spacing** | Wide — roughly midway between face centre and face edge (the 43.9% modal practice) | Close-set eyes were **significantly less intelligent**, described "dumb" and "goofy," and significantly more childlike. Widely spaced eyes were among the features of faces chosen for entertainment |
| **Vertical placement** | Centred or slightly raised; keep the face symmetrical | 38.2% of real faces centre the eyes vertically; the raised-eyes variant drew no penalty on any scale. The authors credit the baseline's trustworthiness partly to "the symmetrical structure of the face and the large, even eyes" |
| **Pupils** | **Non-negotiable.** If exactly one detail goes inside the eye, it is a pupil | Removing pupils made the face significantly less friendly, less trustworthy, less likable and significantly **more machine-like**; "dead eyes robot," "soulless robot," "creepy," and assigned to surveillance. *Caveat: static stimuli; the authors say a blinking pupil-less face may not read as soulless* |
| **Irises** | Add an iris ring if budget allows | Most-liked of all 17 variants; significantly more human-like. "Making the eyes a little more human with the color placement makes it feel quite friendly and approachable" |
| **Catchlight** | Use one, but know it is unevidenced | Coded as a design variable ("reflected eye glare") in the 157-face census, but **no perception result isolates it**. Craft knowledge, not evidence |
| **Eyelids** | Never draw a static lowered lid. Lids exist **only** as blink animation | Drawn eyelids were among the three worst features on friendliness, trust and likability — "sly," "smug" — and also read as masculine. The authors flag this as a possible static-image artefact |
| **Eyebrows** | Short, straight, thin — and ideally absent at rest, appearing transiently for concerned/curious/surprised. Do **not** make brows the anger channel | Brows made the face significantly more human-like and **the most mature of all 17 faces** ("older," "elderly") — but uniquely among extra features they cost nothing on friendliness, trust or likability, and the brow variant was one of the two most popular faces for the **home**. The two friendliest real robots both had short, linear brows. Lowered brows (AU4) are the strongest anger signal available; Gongzi, the least friendly and least trustworthy face in Study 1, was described as having "angry eyes, possibly used for surveillance." **No study isolates eyebrow-only expression** |
| **Mouth** | Include one. A **single animatable line or curve** is enough | MiRAE's one-stroke lower feature plus one-stroke upper feature reached 87–100% on four of five basic emotions, beating much richer faces. 65.6% of real rendered faces have a mouth |
| **When the mouth must show** | Happy, sad and neutral. Fear, anger, surprise and disgust lose nothing without it | In the n=305 study, Fear/Anger/Surprise/Disgust showed **no significant difference** between eye-only and full-face; **Happy, Sad and Neutral were significantly worse from the eyes alone, Happy worst affected** ([arXiv:2410.14337](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14337v1)). **No study tests a mouth that appears only during expression against an always-visible one** — this is the report's central inference, not a finding |
| **Mouth size** | Small | Part of the documented baby-schema cluster: "infantile features — large eyes, large head, small mouth — evoke a nurturing response" |
| **Cheeks / blush** | Avoid | Significantly **less likable** and significantly **less intelligent**; consistently rated feminine, with several participants reading the blush as makeup or eyeliner. Also a culturally specific kawaii convention, not a universal cue |
| **Hair, ears, nose** | Omit | All raise human-likeness, which is the wrong direction for the home context; hair reads masculine; a nose is present in only 21.7% of real faces |
| **Face proportions** | Wide, high-foreheaded, eyes low in the field | The baby-schema replication held only for **face width (p = .018)** and **forehead-to-face-length ratio (p = .014)**, and only for Caucasian faces — a much narrower result than Glocker et al.'s original ([PLOS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4386811/table/pone.0121554.t004)). Treat baby schema as a cluster, not a single dial |
| **Expression vocabulary** | Build happy, sad, surprise/curious and a soft annoyance. **Do not attempt fear or disgust** | Fear has never exceeded 47% on any measured robot face (MiRAE 43%, Kismet 47%, Feelix 16%, BERT 44%); disgust ranges 18–87%. Fear→surprise and disgust→anger are the documented confusions. A minimal face effectively has *one* wide-eyed state — assign it to surprise/curiosity |
| **Amplitude** | Exaggerate well beyond human proportions | Perceived expression strength predicted identification accuracy at **r² = .896**. A minimal face has no micro-detail to carry subtlety; amplitude is the only lever |
| **Body motion** | Pair every expression with a head or body movement | Neck motion took MiRAE from 96.7/100/86.7/96.7% to **100% on happy, sad, anger and surprise**. The cheapest accuracy gain documented anywhere in this literature |
| **Panel sharing** | The display shows the face and **nothing else**, ever | Kuri's rule; NIO's face-integrity rule (it refused even a fatigue-warning teacup); Astro's reviewer-named failure when Alexa pre-empts the eyes |

A gap worth stating outright: **no study measures the legibility of the states a home robot actually needs** — listening, thinking, confused, acknowledging, sleepy, charging. The field measures the Ekman six, which are a poor fit for the real vocabulary. Those states will have to be validated in-house.

## Vector's 43 floats: anger and sadness differ only by a sign

The most implementable rig in the record is Cozmo/Vector's, reverse-engineered in `pycozmo`. It is **procedural, not sprite-based**: the entire face is "a flat list of 43 floats" — **5 global face parameters (indices 0–4), 19 for the left eye (5–23), 19 for the right (24–42)** ([pycozmo procedural_face.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/zayfod/pycozmo/master/pycozmo/procedural_face.py)).

The global five are `center_x 0.0`, `center_y 0.0`, `scale_x 1.0`, `scale_y 1.0`, `angle 0.0`. Each eye's nineteen, by offset: `+0 center_x 0.0`, `+1 center_y 0.0`, `+2 scale_x 1.0`, `+3 scale_y 1.0`, `+4 angle 0.0`; then **eight independent corner radii** — `+5 lower_inner_radius_x`, `+6 lower_inner_radius_y`, `+7 lower_outer_radius_x`, `+8 lower_outer_radius_y`, `+9 upper_inner_radius_x`, `+10 upper_inner_radius_y`, `+11 upper_outer_radius_x`, `+12 upper_outer_radius_y`, all defaulting to 0.5; then two deformable lids — `+13 upper lid y`, `+14 upper lid angle`, `+15 upper lid bend`, `+16 lower lid y`, `+17 lower lid angle`, `+18 lower lid bend`, all defaulting to 0.0.

Read structurally, that is a **2D rig with exactly six capabilities**: translate (`center_x/y`), squash and stretch (`scale_x/y`), rotate (`angle`), silhouette (the 8 corner radii), and occlusion (2 lids × y/angle/bend). There are **no pupils in the model at all** — shape and lid deformation carry the entire emotional load. Any reimplementation reproduces Vector-class expressiveness with those nineteen channels per eye.

Construction detail, for an implementer: the eye body is **five filled rectangles plus four corner pie slices**, with `corner_radius = width/20 + height/10` (12.8 at default resolution). The eye is rotated by `angle`, scaled by `scale_x`/`scale_y`, and pasted with offsets scaled by `X_FACTOR = 0.55`, `Y_FACTOR = 0.25`. Each lid is a rectangle of half-width `1.2 * eye_width` plus a chord of half-width `1.2 * half_eye_width`, drawn on a **2× canvas for antialiasing**; the upper lid uses `y_offset = -half_eye_height, angle_offset = 0.0` and the lower `y_offset = half_eye_height + 1, angle_offset = 180.0`. Defaults: `DEFAULT_WIDTH 128`, `DEFAULT_HEIGHT 64`, `DEFAULT_EYE_WIDTH 28`, `DEFAULT_EYE_HEIGHT 40`, output downsampled `np_im[::2]` to **128 × 32** with nearest-neighbour resampling. Generator bounds: `MAX_X_OFFSET 10.0`, `MAX_Y_OFFSET 50.0`, `MAX_EYE_SCALE 0.25`, `BLINK_SCALE_X 15.0`, `BLINK_SCALE_Y 0.01`. **The source defines no valid ranges for the 43 parameters themselves** — only these generator constants.

The economy of the emotion encoding is the real lesson. `pycozmo` ships **25 named presets** (Amazement, Anger, Annoyance, Asleep, Boredom, Confusion, Despair, Disappointment, Disgust, Embarrassment, Excitement, Fear, Fury, Guilt, Happiness, Horror, Neutral, Pleading, Rejection, Sadness, Skepticism, Surprise, Suspicion, Tiredness, Vulnerability — [docs](https://pycozmo.readthedocs.io/en/latest/generated/pycozmo.expressions.expressions.html)), and **most touch only 2–6 of the 19 channels** ([expressions.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/zayfod/pycozmo/master/pycozmo/expressions/expressions.py)):

- **Happiness** — both eyes `upper_outer_radius_x = 1.0`, `upper_inner_radius_x = 1.0`; `lids[1].y = 0.4`, `lids[1].bend = 0.4`. Top corners fully rounded plus a raised, bowed lower lid: the classic smiling-eye arc.
- **Sadness** — `eyes[0].lids[0].y = 0.6, angle = 20.0`; `eyes[1].lids[0].y = 0.6, angle = -20.0`.
- **Anger** — `eyes[0].lids[0].y = 0.6, angle = -30.0`; `eyes[1].lids[0].y = 0.6, angle = 30.0`. **Identical lid drop to sadness; the entire anger-versus-sadness distinction is the sign of the upper-lid angle.**
- **Surprise** — both eyes `scale_x = 1.25`, `scale_y = 1.25`. Pure uniform +25% stretch.
- **Tiredness** — `lids[0].angle = ±5.0`, `lids[0].y = 0.4`, `lids[1].y = 0.5`.
- **Asleep** — both eyes `center_y = 50.0`, `lids[0].y = 0.45`, `lids[1].y = 0.5`: eyes driven down the screen and nearly fully occluded.
- **Amazement** — `lids[1].y = 0.2` both eyes.
- **Confusion** — deliberately **asymmetric**: `eyes[0].lids[1].y = 0.2, bend = 0.2`; `eyes[1].lids[0].angle = -10.0, y = 0.3`, `eyes[1].lids[1].angle = 5.0, y = 0.2, bend = 0.2`.

The implementation pattern this dictates: build one neutral base vector, define each emotion as a **sparse delta**, and interpolate the whole 43-float vector for transitions — which is what Vector's firmware does, storing keyframe eye settings and moving "each working parameter from frame to frame over a set time period" ([randym32 Vector docs](https://randym32.github.io/Anki.Vector.Documentation/tools/Eye%20animation.html)). Those community docs also name the animator-facing control axes — brow, gaze L/R and U/D, cheeks, eye openness including squint and sleepiness, head tilt, and "squash and stretch (how close or far apart the eyes are)" — plus a joystick tool with four simplified axes: worry↔curiosity, mad↔happy, blink rate slow↔fast, gaze L↔R. The firmware exposes `kProcFace_EllipseDelta` as an integer console variable ranging **1 to 90**.

Three caveats. `pycozmo` is reverse-engineering, not Anki documentation — the parameter *names* are pycozmo's and may not match Anki's internal identifiers. **No source gives Vector's or Cozmo's actual face render frame rate**, nor any numeric blink timing beyond the qualitative "blinking is handled by a timer plus some intermediate steps," nor any numeric emotion-to-emotion transition duration. And Vector's official SDK does not expose the procedural parameters at all: it offers only `set_screen_with_image_data` and `set_screen_to_color` over a **184 × 96 RGB565 framebuffer, 35,328 bytes per frame** ([screen.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/anki/vector-python-sdk/master/anki_vector/screen.py)). Jibo took the opposite architectural route — a library of discrete named clips addressed by **Embodied Speech tags** inlined into the utterance (`<es name='Eye_Curious_01'/>`), with numbered variants as the anti-repetition device ([Jibo ESML reference](https://hri2024.jibo.media.mit.edu/attachments/SDK-SDK---ESML-121023-203758.pdf)). No parameter list, timing, frame rate or resolution for Jibo's face exists in any accessible source, and **the reason for its single eye survives only in an inaccessible video** — do not assert one.

### Timing numbers for aliveness

Humans blink **about 14 to 17 times a minute** ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/body/blinking)) — roughly one every 3.5–4.3 s — rising to ~**26/min in conversation** and collapsing to **3–5/min** while reading or at a screen. A 2008 optoelectronic study found women ~**19/min** against men ~**11/min** ([PubMed 18565090](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18565090/)). The one robot implementation with a measured outcome, iCub's conversational blinking module, used **23.3 blinks/minute**, and human-like blinking made the robot seem **more intelligent** than either no blinking or statistically-timed blinking — while the authors stressed naturalistic blinking alone "is not enough for a robot to meet the requirements for its social integration" ([iCub blinking](https://alecive.github.io/research/2016/11/02/iCubBlinking/)). Human blink frequency follows a **log-normal distribution**, so sample intervals rather than using a fixed period.

FluxGarage RoboEyes, the most completely documented shipping library, defaults to `blinkInterval = 1 s` plus `blinkIntervalVariation = 4 s` — **a 1–4 s window, i.e. 15–60 blinks/min**, deliberately livelier than human. Its idle re-gaze (the saccade analogue) runs `idleInterval = 1 s` + `idleIntervalVariation = 3 s`, a **1–3 s window**. Macro animations are **500 ms** (`anim_confused()`, a horizontal shake of amplitude 20; `anim_laugh()`, vertical, amplitude 5). Micro-jitter is exposed as `hFlickerAmplitude = 2 px` and `vFlickerAmplitude = 10 px` ([FluxGarage_RoboEyes.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/FluxGarage/RoboEyes/main/src/FluxGarage_RoboEyes.h)). The SunFounder tutorial's `setAutoblinker(ON, 3, 2)` — base 3 s plus up to 2 s random — is the setting that maps onto the human 14–17/min baseline, and is the number to copy ([SunFounder](https://docs.sunfounder.com/projects/summary-of-fun-projects/en/latest/arduino/roboeyes1.0.html)). The reusable pattern is the **two-level timer: fixed base plus random variation, never a fixed period**, or the face reads as a metronome.

Blink *dynamics* have the one piece of hard animation research. Disney Research and CMU found blinks generated from a model of real human blink data, **with fully closing eyelids**, were consistently perceived as more natural than blinks built from the dynamics proposed in animation textbooks ([Modeling and Animating Eye Blinks](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/Modeling-and-Animating-Eye-Blinks-Paper.pdf)). So: the lid must fully close, and the close should be faster than the open.

Gaze must **snap, not tween**. Saccades last **20–200 ms depending on amplitude**, with 20–30 ms typical in reading, microsaccades ~20 ms, and the largest reaching 300 ms ([Wikipedia: Saccade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saccade)); a visual-search task measured a mean of **36.6 ms (SD 14.4)** ([arXiv:2004.02873](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.02873)). At 50 fps that is **one to two frames** — essentially instantaneous. Smoothly tweening a gaze shift over many frames is the commonest tell of an unconvincing robot eye. Lid and shape changes ease; gaze snaps.

For "thinking," **avert gaze upward, then return**. Andrist et al. built gaze aversion into a NAO from human conversational statistics: people look away to signal thinking, usually **upward**, and well-timed aversions produced better conversational-floor management and ratings of being **more thoughtful** — though users did *not* report more comfort with a gaze-averting robot, which Andrist called surprising. (This study was reached only through secondary summary; the primary citation could not be confirmed.) And do not hold constant mutual gaze: Koller et al. (2023), **n=101** with a Pepper robot varying the gaze-aversion ratio across its full range, conclude that **high mutual gaze is not always a sign of high comfort** ([Frontiers in Robotics and AI](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2023.1062714)).

Frame rate: the one hard documented number is RoboEyes' **50 fps default** (`frameInterval = 20 ms`), settable via `begin(width, height, frameRate)`, with `update()` as a non-blocking `millis()` gate so the face degrades gracefully to whatever the bus allows rather than stuttering. **No source gives a minimum frame rate at which a robot face reads as smooth, and no perceptual study on the question exists.** Reasoning from the numbers above rather than from evidence: a blink of 100–150 ms resolves into 5–8 frames at 50 fps, which is about the floor for reading as motion; at 20–25 fps it would be 2–3 frames and likely read as a glitch. **Treat ~30 fps as a practical floor and 50 fps as the target — as an inference, not a finding.** Finally, two free details worth copying: RoboEyes initialises `eyeHeightCurrent = 1`, so every boot is an opening-eyes "waking up" beat; and no documented breathing-cycle numbers exist for any robot face, so if a breath idle is added (human resting respiration is ~12–20/min), flag it as invention.

For indicating speech without lip-sync, the honest answer is that **no documented eye-based "lip-sync" substitute exists** — no pulsing eyes, no lid oscillation keyed to amplitude. That is a genuine hole in the literature. What is evidenced is the co-speech route: a puppetry-principles paper finds puppeteers hold that movement should follow "the emotion, the listener, and the rhythm of the dialogue," that the speech cue can come from the head or whole body instead of a mouth, and that non-humanoid robots have signalled speech with lights and whole-body movement — which helped people identify **which robot in a group was talking** ([arXiv:2607.03289](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.03289)). Architecturally, Jibo's ESML and Anki's co-streamed audio-plus-animation are the same idea: **tag the utterance, don't analyse the waveform.** Driving a subtle eye-scale or lid oscillation from the speech envelope is a reasonable proposal but is documented nowhere.

## What the Pi can actually drive, and why the 1.28in round panel is the wrong choice

Full-frame SPI refresh is bandwidth-bound and scales inversely with pixel count. The benchmark set from `fbcp-ili9341` is unambiguous: **240 × 240 ST7789 panels hit 91.69–92.23 fps** at an obtained bus of 84.5–85 MHz; a 240 × 320 ILI9341 PiTFT 2.8in hits **59.81 fps**; and 320 × 480 panels collapse — PiTFT 3.5in HX8357D at **21.29 fps**, Waveshare 3.5in ILI9486 at **12.97 fps**, KeDei MPI3501 at **4.8 fps** ([fbcp-ili9341](https://github.com/juj/fbcp-ili9341)). The governing rule: a 320 × 240 16bpp display needs about **73.728 MHz of SPI clock to reach 60 fps**. Bit depth dominates bandwidth gains — the ILI9486L got 56.8% more bus bandwidth than the ILI9486 and barely improved, because it needs 24 bits per pixel against 16. ILI9341 panels nominally rated 10 MHz (datasheet 25 MHz) were routinely overclocked to **70–80 MHz** and ran fine, which is out-of-spec behaviour that happens to work. Watch the clock governor: Pi 3B and Zero W default to a 400 MHz core but **drop to 250 MHz under no load, cutting SPI bandwidth ~60%**.

**Those numbers are now historical.** The February 2024 update to that driver states "the era of fbcp-ili9341 has come to an end": it was built on the deprecated VideoCore DispmanX API, which is unavailable on Pi 5 and later, no longer enabled by default even on Pi 0–4, and **it only ever worked on 32-bit operating systems**. Raspberry Pi moved to the KMS compositor stack. The best modern figure available is a notro GUD mipi-dbi test on a Pi 4 at 62.5 MHz SPI: about **40.8 fps for a 320 × 240 RGB565 frame** uncompressed, SPI-bus-limited rather than CPU-limited ([notro gist](https://gist.github.com/notro/1fbd5df09c71261d0d7773a755ac9ee8)). **No post-KMS, 64-bit, Pi 5 benchmark exists for any SPI panel, and no GC9A01 benchmark exists on a Pi at all** — the 240 × 240 ST7789 figures are the closest proxy. Practical read: a **240 × 240 face at 30 fps is comfortably achievable** over SPI even on the modern kernel path; a **320 × 480 face at 30 fps is not**, with full-frame updates. Dirty-rectangle updates are the single biggest lever available, since a character face is mostly static background with small moving regions — but **no measured figures for partial-update throughput were found**, so that multiplier is an inference.

**The Waveshare 1.28in round GC9A01 is the wrong panel for a shelf device, and the reason is physical, not computational.** Its 240 × 240 resolution is perfectly adequate; its **active area is Φ32.4 mm — about the size of a large coin** — with a 0.135 mm pixel pitch, at **$14.99** ([Waveshare](https://www.waveshare.com/product/displays/lcd-oled/1.28inch-lcd-module.htm)). Applying Daktronics' vendor rule of thumb (optimal viewing distance in feet ≈ 3× pixel pitch in mm) gives an optimal distance of roughly **0.4 m** ([Daktronics](https://www.daktronics.com/en-us/support/kb/000030569)). Viewed from 2–4 m across a living room, the viewer sits five to ten times past that: individual pixels become irrelevant and the whole panel subtends a tiny visual angle. **Resolution is never the constraint; angular size of the physical panel is.** The signage literature compounds the point — legibility distance keeps improving with feature height while contrast and stroke width plateau ([US Access Board](https://www.access-board.gov/research/communication/variable-message-signing/vms-legibility/)), and an FHWA study found a 20 mm pixel-pitch display out-reads a 1.6-inch-pitch predecessor ([FHWA](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/15027/004.cfm)).

The realistic floor is therefore **Pimoroni's HyperPixel 2.1 Round** — 480 × 480 minus corners, ~229 PPI, 2.1in IPS, 18-bit colour, 175° viewing angle, **60 FPS**, active area **53.28 × 53.28 mm**, ~AU$109.95 — or a 2.8in-plus panel ([Pimoroni](https://shop.pimoroni.com/products/hyperpixel-round)). Note the HyperPixel Round's software caveat: recent Raspberry Pi OS releases do not fully support it and users are directed to a provided Bookworm image ([The Pi Hut](https://thepihut.com/collections/raspberry-pi-hats/products/hyperpixel-2-1-round-hi-res-display-for-raspberry-pi)). Choosing DSI or DPI sidesteps the bandwidth problem entirely — these behave as a normal Linux framebuffer at 60 fps. The Raspberry Pi Touch Display 2 is **$40 for 5in / $60 for 7in**, both 1280 × 720, powered from GPIO, in production until at least January 2030, compatible with every Pi from 1B+ onward **except the Zero series, which has no DSI port**; the 10.1in needs four DSI lanes and is **not compatible with Pi 4 or earlier** ([Raspberry Pi](https://raspberrypi.com/products/touch-display-2); [CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/2025/08/18/raspberry-pi-touch-display-2-gets-40-5-inch-variant-with-1280x720-resolution/)). Waveshare's 2.8in DSI is 480 × 640 portrait IPS at 160° ([Waveshare](https://www.waveshare.com/product/raspberry-pi/2.8inch-dsi-lcd.htm)). **The viewing-angle figures do not reconcile**: Waveshare lists 170° for the 7in Touch Display 2 while Tom's Hardware gives 85° for the 7in and 80° for the 5in ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/uk/raspberry-pi/raspberry-pi-releases-smaller-five-inch-touch-display-2-lower-in-price-same-resolution-as-larger-model)) — most likely a half-angle versus full-angle convention, but the sources are not reconciled and should not be presented as settled. E-paper is disqualified outright: best-case **0.3 s partial refresh is ~3 fps**, full refresh takes 2–4 s, and manuals mandate a full refresh after several partials, producing a visible multi-second flash ([Waveshare wiki](https://waveshare.com/wiki/2.13inch_e-Paper_HAT%2B)).

On stroke width, the ADA-referenced low-resolution variable-message-sign table sanctions a **1-pixel stroke only for features ~7 px tall**, with 10–12 px features requiring 2-pixel strokes ([Access Board, Table 11B-703.8.5](https://www.corada.com/documents/2016CBCPG/11b-703-8-variable-message-signs)). A face viewed at 2–4 m is far past reading distance, so strokes should scale well beyond the table: a practical floor is roughly **4% of the display's smaller dimension (≈8–10 px at 240 × 240)** for any line that must read across a room. **Never draw a 1 px stroke on a face.** Thin lines fail twice over: at these pitches a 1–2 px line subtends well under one arcminute at 3 m, below the ~1 arcminute 20/20 acuity limit the NIST relation implies ([NIST TN 1180](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/TN/nbstechnicalnote1180.pdf)), and anti-aliasing spreads its already-marginal contrast across neighbours. Hard-edged, thick, high-contrast shapes filling a large fraction of the panel are the only forms that survive. Note the honest limit here: **every quantitative legibility source found is about text characters. No research exists on the legibility of faces or icons at distance** — the transfer is an inference.

### OLED versus LCD for a glowing face on black

The optical case for OLED is structural and specific to this design. An LCD's "black" is a lit backlight imperfectly blocked by liquid crystal, so the field around the face is dark grey and, in a dim room, visibly glowing with a halo around every bright feature. On OLED a black pixel emits nothing, so the face appears to float. **For a glowing-face-on-black aesthetic, OLED is not a marginal upgrade — it is the whole look.**

Burn-in is real but tractable at this scale. It "won't likely occur after just a few hours"; tests indicate permanent burn-in follows **months or years** of static content, and in one RTINGS long-term test **subtitles caused burn-in after roughly 7,100 hours** ([Engadget](https://engadget.com/2203219/oled-burn-in-risks-explained/); [RTINGS](https://www.rtings.com/tv/learn/real-life-oled-burn-in-test)) — at 8 h/day, about 2.4 years of *continuous static* content. Brightness is the dominant factor: the maximum-brightness test set burned in more severely than the 200-nit set. Cumulative duration is what counts and splitting it does not help — LG says 30 minutes twice a day equals one hour once a day ([Engadget](https://engadget.com/2246417/tvs-at-risk-of-burn-in/)). The **red sub-pixel degrades fastest, then blue, then green**. Newer panels are not inherently safer; RTINGS found 2020–2023 OLEDs did not consistently outperform a 2017 LG C7, and frames their extra brightness headroom as an opportunity to reduce output rather than a fix ([RTINGS](https://www.rtings.com/tv/learn/oled-burn-in-not-improved-as-expected)). The mitigations map neatly onto a character face: the face is intrinsically animated (varied content, the low-risk category); add **micro-drift**, translating the whole face a few pixels on a slow cycle, which is pixel shift in one line of blit offset; run at **moderate brightness**, the strongest single lever; **avoid saturated red** for large persistent features; and blank or dim when nobody is present.

Two counterweights. First, **every burn-in source found is TV and monitor panels, not small SSD1306/SSD1351 PMOLED modules**, which use different materials and drive schemes; no hours-to-half-brightness figure was found for the specific modules. That is a meaningful extrapolation gap. Second, colour OLED at this size is small and expensive: the SSD1351 option is 1.5in/128 × 128 at **$39.95** ([Adafruit 1431](https://www.adafruit.com/products/1431)) against $14.99 for a larger-resolution LCD. Since angular size is the real legibility constraint, the builder may be forced to choose a bigger LCD over a better-looking small OLED. For a **lit** living room a backlit IPS LCD at high brightness is the safer bet; for a **dim** room OLED wins decisively, because OLED without a backlight is typically dimmer in daylight. An LCD halo can be partly tamed with a dark bezel, a neutral-density or AR front layer, and keeping the face's own luminance high. And the single largest hole in the hardware evidence: **no brightness figure in nits was found for any surveyed panel** — not the GC9A01 module, not HyperPixel Round, not Touch Display 2 — so "is it readable in a lit living room?" cannot be answered quantitatively at all. Nor was any measured contrast ratio, any power-consumption figure in mA, or any off-the-shelf enclosure: Waveshare offers cases only for its 4.3in and 7in DSI panels, and **no vendor sells a Pi face-device housing**. That is a negative finding, not a search failure — plan on 3D printing.

One format note. On a round panel the circular mask is a hard constraint: anything near the corners of the 240 × 240 buffer is simply invisible, leaving π/4 ≈ **79% of the raster usable**. A round panel therefore argues for one large cyclops eye — where the circle *is* the iris, extremely legible because the feature fills the panel — or a whole circular head. Two eyes plus a mouth inside one circle wastes the format. A **320 × 240 landscape panel is the only shape where a conventional eyes-plus-mouth layout is efficient**, which, given the recommendation below, is the shape to buy. RoboEyes supports `setCyclops(true)` if the round route is taken, and Adafruit's Snake Eyes Bonnet establishes the alternative two-panel pattern (one display per eye, split across the Pi's dual SPI buses) at the cost of no mouth and a harder enclosure ([Adafruit](https://learn.adafruit.com/animated-snake-eyes-bonnet-for-raspberry-pi/overview)).

## No one has measured whether Indian viewers read eyes or mouths

The eyes-versus-mouth cultural split is well established — for Japan against the US and the Netherlands, and nowhere else relevant here. Yuki, Maddux & Masuda (2007) found Japanese participants gave **more weight to the eyes** when judging emotion while Americans gave **more weight to the mouth**, with the mechanism being that eyes are harder to control voluntarily, so cultures that discourage overt display attend to them ([Yuki et al. 2007](https://willmaddux.web.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/15846/2018/04/JESP-Face-Paper.pdf)). The directly design-relevant evidence is the emoticon finding: Japanese emoticons for happiness and sadness **vary in how the eyes are depicted** (^_^, ;_;, >_<) while American ones **vary with the direction of the mouth** (:-), :-(). A later Japan-versus-Netherlands study replicated the pattern ([Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022022117734372)). The popular extension to anime eye sizes is commentary, not a finding, and was never tested by the study.

**State this plainly: no study places Indian participants on this axis, and none examines Indian users' robot-face preferences.** Generalising along a chain from Japan to "East Asian" to "Asian" to "Indian" is unwarranted — India sits in neither the Japanese nor the American cluster on the collectivism and display-rule dimensions the theory invokes. Kalegina's corpus is itself culturally skewed (33.8% US-origin robots) and the authors list perceived robot ethnicity as future work. Robot appearance preferences are known to be culture-dependent in general (Li, Rau & Li 2010, verified only as cited in Kalegina). Bennett & Šabanović planned a Japan–US cross-cultural minimal-face study for spring 2013; no results were found, and it is unclear whether it was published.

**Redundant encoding is the engineering answer precisely because it is robust to being wrong about which channel dominates.** Change *both* eye shape and mouth for every state; never let two states be distinguishable by the mouth alone or the eye alone. That means giving the eyes real **shape-change** capability, not merely gaze direction — arc, squint, widen, narrow — which is exactly the channel the Japanese emoticon convention reads, and exactly what Vector's eight corner radii and two deformable lids provide. A secondary consequence: skip pink blush as a happiness signal. Beyond its measured likability, intelligence and gender costs, it is a specific kawaii convention rather than a universally read cue. The cheapest high-value primary research this project could do is a small eye-versus-mouth forced-choice emoticon study with Marathi, Hindi and English-speaking Indian participants, replicating Yuki et al.'s method.

### Licence hazards before you vendor anything

**FluxGarage RoboEyes is GPL-3.0** (current release 1.1.1, dated 2025-09-03, depending on Adafruit GFX — [Arduino Libraries](https://www.arduinolibraries.info/libraries/flux-garage-robo-eyes)). It is the most directly reusable and best-documented project in the field — full parameter set, 50 fps default, four moods (`DEFAULT/TIRED/ANGRY/HAPPY`), 8-way gaze (`N/NE/E/SE/S/SW/W/NW`), geometry setters (`setWidth`, `setHeight`, `setBorderradius`, `setSpacebetween`), behaviour toggles (`setAutoblinker`, `setIdleMode`, `setCuriosity`, `setCyclops`, `setHFlicker`/`setVFlicker`, `setSweat`), defaults of 128 × 64 screen with 36 × 36 eyes, border radius 8 and spacing 10 — and linking its **code** into a closed product is not viable. Its **parameter design is freely imitable; its code is not.** Treat it as a specification to reimplement, not a dependency.

**Adafruit Uncanny Eyes has no LICENSE file** and no licence field on the repository, whose file list shows only `convert`, `uncannyEyes` and `README.md` ([github.com/adafruit/Uncanny_Eyes](https://github.com/adafruit/Uncanny_Eyes)). That is the worst case: **no grant at all**, despite Adafruit normally using MIT/BSD. Permission would have to be confirmed directly. It is also a different design philosophy — a photorealistic eyeball renderer with swappable appearance headers (`defaultEye.h`, `noSclera.h`, `dragonEye.h`, `goatEye.h`) and iris/pupil/sclera parameters including `IRIS_MIN`/`IRIS_MAX` (0–1023), `IRIS_SMOOTH`, `TRACKING` and `AUTOBLINK` ([Adafruit software page](https://learn.adafruit.com/animated-electronic-eyes/software)) — not the abstract-shape model this project wants. No frame rate is documented for it.

**Anki's `vector-python-sdk` is Apache 2.0** and safe to vendor — but it is framebuffer-only (`set_screen_with_image_data`, `set_screen_to_color`), so it grants no procedural face. **The 43-float procedural parameterisation reaches you only through `pycozmo`, which is reverse-engineered** from Cozmo animation data, not Anki documentation; verify its licence terms before vendoring and treat the parameter names as pycozmo's own. Also unverified: `ggldnl/procedural-expression-library` (not inspected — parameters and licence unknown), the Reachy Mini community eye mod (licence unverified), and `bot_ekko` (licence not established). The Hackster "Robot Eyes" project is reported MIT-licensed driving a 1.8in ST7735 on a Pi, but the page returned HTTP 403 and the detail comes from search metadata only.

## What this adds up to

The industry-versus-lab contradiction dissolves once you notice that the two bodies of evidence answer different questions. The lab asked *what does a still picture of a face communicate*, and answered: pupils and a mouth, or it reads as a surveillance camera. The industry asked *what can a 23-millimetre panel on a moving head communicate*, and answered: two enormous shapes that squash, stretch and blink. **Neither answer generalises to a 2.8-inch panel sitting still on a shelf, which is the problem actually at hand — and that case has never been studied.** The honest position is that this project operates in an evidential gap and must hedge against it, which is what redundant encoding across eyes and mouth does. Build the Vector-style nineteen-channel per-eye rig because its shape vocabulary is the best-proven abstract-eye parameterisation in existence and because it supplies the eye-shape channel an eye-weighting viewer needs; add visible pupils, because that is the single most damaging omission anyone has measured and the cost is two filled circles; add one animatable mouth stroke, present for happy, sad and neutral and free to retract for surprise, curiosity and annoyance, because that is where the recognition data says a mouth earns its pixels and where it does not. NIO's mouthlessness was an argument against lip-sync, not against mouths — honour the first and ignore the second.

Three things matter more than any of the feature decisions. **Motion beats features**: neck motion alone took a two-stroke face from 87% to 100% on anger, and no static finding in this literature survives contact with an animated face — so the engineering budget belongs in blink dynamics, snapping saccades, log-normal idle timers and a physical or simulated head motion long before it goes to a nose or eyebrows. **The panel must only ever be a face**: Kuri articulated the rule, NIO independently reinvented it as face integrity, and Astro is the shipped counter-example whose reviewers named the violation as the thing that broke the character. And **a face amplifies both success and failure** — in the NOMI study, going offline hurt the experience even with the face present, and experienced assistant users dismissed it as "essentially the MBUX assistant with a robot face" ([arXiv:2402.11853v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.11853v2), n=10, single country, self-report, with p-values that do not fully match its own tables — a weak study carrying a lot of weight in this literature, and the only direct faced-versus-faceless manipulation anyone has run). The expressive premium a face buys is relational, not functional: it purchases warmth, engagement and companionship, and purchases nothing in perceived competence. Make the assistant work offline first, then give it a face — and if it must share a shelf with an always-listening microphone, remember that anthropomorphism is documented to lower *felt* privacy risk rather than actual risk, which is an ethical exposure as much as a design one.
