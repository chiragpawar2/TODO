# Designing a Minimal Robot Face That Reads as Friendly, Trustworthy and Alive — Academic & Design Literature

Scope note: the single most directly applicable study found is Kalegina, Schroeder, Allchin, Berlin & Cakmak, "Characterizing the Design Space of Rendered Robot Faces," HRI 2018 (Best Paper nominee). It is a survey of 157 screen-rendered robot faces coded on 76 attributes, plus two perception studies — one on 12 real robot faces, one on 17 synthesized faces that differ from a baseline by exactly one feature. That one-feature-at-a-time design is what makes it directly usable for concrete decisions. Full text: [Kalegina et al. 2018, HRI (PDF)](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf) / [ACM DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3171221.3171286). Live dataset at robotfaces.org.

---

## 1. The uncanny valley: state of evidence, and where stylised/minimal faces sit

### Takeaway
The uncanny valley is real enough to design around but the evidence is messier than the famous curve implies: it replicates reliably for photorealistic/near-human faces and poorly for cartoon-morph stimuli. Stylised minimal faces sit safely on the left of the curve — the empirical risk for a minimal face is not uncanniness from realism, it is *creepiness from omission* (missing pupils, missing mouth), which is a different mechanism.

### Cited Findings
- Reviews have repeatedly found the empirical evidence inconsistent: a 2015 review concluded evidence "remained inconsistent," and two earlier reviews concluded the effect was either absent or inconsistent — [Kätsyri et al. 2015, review of uncanny valley hypotheses (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4392592/). A later meta-analysis by Diel et al. confirmed the effect's statistical significance and estimated its effect size — [Diel et al. meta-analysis (Cardiff ORCA PDF)](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/148120/1/Diel.%20A%20meta-analysis%20of%20the%20uncanny.pdf). These are in tension: the effect exists on aggregate but individual studies often fail to find it.
- A 2018 Heliyon study failed to replicate the valley using morphed CGI robot–human images, but *did* find it with photorealistic, non-morphed robot photographs; the authors attribute replication difficulty to the lack of pre-validated stimulus sets — [Heliyon 2018 (DOAJ record)](https://doaj.org/article/81da6ae355e147fb95f461f3a0cecb22).
- Seyama & Nagayama (2007) found that morphing human faces produced an uncanny valley *only when the faces had abnormal features* — i.e. the trigger is feature abnormality/mismatch, not realism per se — as summarised in [Kätsyri et al. 2015 (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4392592/). That review's own conclusion is that "perceptual mismatch" is the best-supported single road to eeriness.
- Yamada et al. (2013) *did* find an uncanny valley effect for cartoon faces morphed with real faces, but not for two morphed real faces — again summarised in [Kätsyri et al. 2015 (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4392592/).
- An EEG study of stylised character faces found the face-specific N170 response was *stronger for both the most abstract and the most realistic* faces than for medium-stylised ones — a U-shape in neural face processing rather than a simple valley — [reported in the 2024 stylisation literature (Essex repository PDF)](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/39955/1/1-s2.0-S107158192400226X-main.pdf).
- A 2024 study measuring uncanniness across cartoon, CG, drawing, real and robot faces found that *specialisation* (how stylised the face category is) significantly moderated the linear effect of distortion on uncanniness, and this model fit the data better than the conventional valley model — [Cardiff ORCA 2024 PDF](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/167947/3/1-s2.0-S0747563224001225-main.pdf). Design reading: within a stylised face category, *internal distortion/inconsistency* is what creates eeriness.
- A 2024 Essex study using implicit measures found the valley for individual robot faces, arguing the measurement instruments used in earlier studies partly explain mixed results — [Essex repository PDF](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/39955/1/1-s2.0-S107158192400226X-main.pdf).
- In Kalegina et al.'s own survey of real robot faces, the faces users called creepy were the highly realistic ones: participant comments included that the more realistic the faces are the creepier they look, and the highly realistic Valerie the Roboceptionist and EMC received low likability ratings explicitly attributed by the authors to the uncanny valley — [Kalegina et al. 2018 (PDF), §4.3](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- Crucially, the friendliest-rated faces in that study (Yumi, FURo-D, Buddy, Datou) "had relatively detailed faces, but were not exceedingly realistic, thus avoiding the Uncanny Valley effect" — [Kalegina et al. 2018 (PDF), §4.3.1](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- Participants preferred *less realistic and less detailed* robots for the **home** context, but highly detailed yet not exceedingly realistic faces for **service** jobs — [Kalegina et al. 2018 (PDF), abstract & §4](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- Broadbent et al. found that even an "uncanny" silver face display promoted perceptions of agency relative to a *no-face* display — i.e. having some face beats having none, even a bad one — as cited in [Kalegina et al. 2018 §2](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Design rule: aim for "moderately detailed, clearly non-realistic."** The empirical sweet spot in the only large rendered-face perception study is detail *without* realism, and for a home product the preference is explicitly toward the less-realistic, less-detailed end of that band.
- **Design rule: the creepiness risk for a minimal face is omission, not realism.** Keep the face internally consistent in stylisation level; do not mix one photoreal element (e.g. a realistic iris texture or human eyelashes) into an otherwise flat geometric face — the perceptual-mismatch account predicts that is exactly what generates eeriness.
- Treat "no uncanny valley risk because we're stylised" as only half true: Yamada's cartoon-morph result and Kalegina's no-pupil "dead eyes / soulless" reactions show stylised faces *can* be rated eerie.

### Gaps
- No study found that places a *specific* minimal 2-dot-eye face on a measured uncanny-valley curve against stylised alternatives. The curve's x-axis position for minimal faces is inferred, not measured.
- Diel et al.'s reported effect size could not be verified in the primary PDF text within this research budget.

## 2. Feature count: which facial features are necessary?

### Takeaway
More features = more human-like (DiSalvo et al. 2002, ~4+ features as the threshold), but human-likeness is *not* the goal — likability is, and Kalegina's controlled single-feature study found that removing **pupils** or the **mouth** is catastrophic for friendliness, trust and likability, while every other feature is roughly optional. So: eyes with pupils + a mouth is the necessary minimum; everything else is a style choice.

### Cited Findings — DiSalvo, Gemperle, Forlizzi & Kiesler (DIS 2002)
- The study analysed **48 humanoid robots**, coding each head for eyes, ears, nose, mouth, eyelids, eyebrows and total feature count, and **scaled every head to 10 inches** so measurements were comparable. Two surveys were run (one showing full robots, one heads only), with **20 participants each**, rating human-likeness on a 1–5 scale — [DiSalvo, Gemperle, Forlizzi & Kiesler, DIS 2002 (dblp record)](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/ACMdis/DiSalvoGFK02.html); survey-summary details per secondary sources including [the full-text PDF host](https://scispace.com/pdf/all-robots-are-not-created-equal-the-design-and-perception-3v2ckzpgxk.pdf).
- Their conclusion: the presence of certain features, the **dimensions of the head**, and the **total number of facial features** all heavily influence perceived humanness; the more features a robot head has, the more human-like it is perceived to be — [DiSalvo et al. 2002, DIS (dblp)](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/ACMdis/DiSalvoGFK02.html).
- Specific features and proportions: a **"portrait" aspect ratio (head taller than wide)**, multiple facial features, and specifically the presence of **nose, mouth and eyelids** increased perceived humanness; heads with a **landscape aspect ratio** and minimal features were seen as robotic — per secondary summary of the paper (see caveat in Gaps).
- Kalegina et al. explicitly state their results "support the design recommendations of DiSalvo et al.": **increasing the complexity of the eyes (e.g. adding irises) and having a face with four or more features increases the perception of humanness** of a robotic head — [Kalegina et al. 2018 (PDF), §5.3.4](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf). This is the citable version of the "~4 features" threshold.

### Cited Findings — what features actual robot faces have (157-face census)
All from [Kalegina et al. 2018 (PDF), §3.3](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf):
- **65.6% have a mouth**; 40.8% eyebrows; 21.7% a nose; 21.0% cheeks/blush; 8.3% hair; 3.8% ears.
- **71.3% have pupils**; 65.6% have **no** eyelids; 59.9% have **no** irises. (So the modal rendered robot face is: eyes with pupils, no iris, no eyelid, plus a mouth, and no nose.)
- Face colour: 34.4% black, 20.4% white, 14.0% blue. Eye colour: 47.1% white, 18.5% blue, 16.6% black.
- Eye shape: 40.8% circular, 28.0% vertical ovals, 11.5% human-eye-shaped.
- Corpus demographics: 157 robots built 2001–2017; 33.8% US, 17.8% China, 10.2% Japan, 8.3% Korea, 2.5% Germany; 60.5% humanoid, 27.4% mechanical, 12.1% zoomorphic; 21.0% built primarily for the **home**.

### Cited Findings — controlled single-feature effects (Study 2, n=50)
Baseline = the computed "average face" of the 157-face dataset; 16 variants each differing by one feature; paired t-tests with Bonferroni correction. All from [Kalegina et al. 2018 (PDF), §5.3](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf):
- **Significantly LESS friendly than baseline:** no mouth, no pupils, eyelids. No variant was significantly *more* friendly than the baseline.
- **Significantly LESS trustworthy:** the same three — eyelids, no mouth, no pupils. No variant was significantly more trustworthy than baseline.
- **Significantly LESS likable:** no mouth, no pupils, cheeks, small eyes, white face, eyelids — with **no-mouth, no-pupils and eyelids receiving the lowest ratings of the whole set**. The most-liked overall (though not significantly above baseline) was the **iris** variant.
- **Significantly LESS intelligent:** no mouth, closely spaced eyes, cheeks. Close-set eyes drew the comments "dumb" and "goofy."
- **Significantly MORE human-like:** ears, eyebrows, hair, irises, nose. **Significantly more machine-like:** no mouth, no pupils.
- **Significantly MORE childlike:** cheeks, smaller eye distance, hair, nose. **Significantly MORE mature:** eyebrows (rated most mature overall; described as "older," "elderly").
- **Gendering is a real risk:** pink/cartoon cheeks were consistently rated feminine across both studies (several participants read the blush as *makeup/eyeliner*); white face was second-most feminine; **eyelids and hair** read as masculine.
- Cross-study convergence: "the faces with no pupils and no mouth were consistently ranked as unfriendly, machinelike, and unlikable, and were overwhelmingly selected for security-type work," with participants volunteering surveillance framings unprompted — [§6 Discussion](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- Robots with at least a pupil and somewhat detailed blue eyes were frequently rated friendly and relatively trustworthy and chosen for entertainment contexts — [§6](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- The four robots rated highest in **likability** were also the four rated **friendliest**; robots high in likability were also rated high in friendliness, trustworthiness and intelligence — [§4.3.7](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf). Li, Rau & Li (2010) found the same coupling of likability and trust.

### Inferences
- **Concrete rule — the non-negotiable minimum is: two eyes, each with a visible pupil, plus a mouth.** These are the only two features whose *absence* was significantly damaging on friendliness, trust, likability and machine-likeness simultaneously.
- **Concrete rule — target roughly 3–4 features, not more.** Four-plus features buys human-likeness (DiSalvo; confirmed by Kalegina), but human-likeness is not what you want for a home product, where users preferred less realism and less detail. Eyes + pupils + mouth is three; adding *irises* is the single highest-value fourth addition (most-liked variant overall, raises human-likeness without the maturity/gender side effects of eyebrows, hair or cheeks).
- **Concrete rule — avoid drawn eyelids on a static face.** Eyelids were among the three worst features on friendliness, trust and likability; participants called the eyelid face "sly" and "smug." The authors note this may be a stimulus artefact (lowered lids read as a sly squint), so a *fully-open* lid or a lid used only as a blink animation is a different proposition — see §6 below.
- **Avoid blush/cheeks and hair** unless you intend to gender the character: both carried strong gender readings, and cheeks also cost likability and perceived intelligence.
- **Do not add a nose for human-likeness reasons** — it raises human-likeness and childlikeness but it is also the feature DiSalvo found pushes toward realism, and it is present in only 21.7% of real rendered faces. If you add one, a small "button" nose reads as babyish (Zebrowitz, *Reading Faces*, cited by Kalegina).

### Gaps
- I could not open the DiSalvo et al. 2002 full text (PDF hosts returned unreadable/empty content). The head aspect-ratio claim and the "nose, mouth, eyelids increase humanness" claim come from secondary summaries, and the exact measured thresholds (e.g. precise width-to-height ratio cutoffs, exact eye-width-to-head-width figures from the 10-inch-normalised measurements) could not be verified. **The "four or more features" threshold is, however, verified** via Kalegina et al.'s direct citation of DiSalvo.
- Kalegina's Study 2 has two acknowledged limitations the designer should weigh: **all stimuli were static images** (the authors note a pupil-less face "may not appear as 'soulless' if it is able to blink"), and **possible co-dependency between features** — the no-mouth face was shown among 16 smiling faces, which may have exaggerated the reaction.

## 3. Eye design specifics: size, spacing, pupils, catchlights, baby schema

### Takeaway
Within the controlled study, **small eyes and close-set eyes are measurably harmful** (less likable; less intelligent / "goofy") while large, widely-spaced eyes with visible pupils are the safe choice — which aligns with baby-schema evidence. The baby-schema effect itself is experimentally established (Glocker et al. 2009) though partially replicated.

### Cited Findings
- **Eye size relative to face:** in the 157-face census, **more than half (51.6%) of real rendered robot faces have eyes occupying between 1/20 and 1/2 of the screen space** — a very wide but usable band, with the modal practice being conspicuously large eyes — [Kalegina et al. 2018 §3.3.2](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- **Small eyes are penalised:** the "small eyes" variant was *significantly less likable* than baseline — [§5.3.7](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf). Notably the small-eye face was **not** perceived as older, which the authors flag as only partial support for the baby-schema prediction.
- **Eye spacing:** 43.9% of surveyed faces space eyes evenly between the centre and the edges of the face; 38.2% have eyes centred vertically on the face — [§3.3.2](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- **Close-set eyes are penalised:** the close-eyes variant was *significantly less intelligent* than baseline and described as "dumb" and "goofy," and was relegated to entertainment roles; it was also rated significantly more **childlike** — [§5.3.2, §5.3.5](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf). **Widely spaced eyes**, by contrast, were among the features of faces most often chosen for entertainment alongside nose and irises — [§5.3.8](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- **Pupils are the single highest-stakes eye decision:** removing pupils made the face significantly less friendly, less trustworthy, less likable and significantly more machine-like; participants named it "dead eyes robot" and "soulless robot" and called it "creepy," and commented that "the bright eyes make the robot appear to be looking for everything" — [§5.3.1–5.3.4, §6](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- **Irises:** the iris variant was the most-liked face overall (not significantly above baseline) and was significantly more human-like; a participant explained that "making the eyes a little more human with the color placement makes it feel quite friendly and approachable" — [§5.3.4, §5.3.7](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- **Catchlight / specular highlight:** Kalegina et al. coded "reflected eye glare" as one of their 76 attributes — confirming it is a recognised design variable in real robot faces — but the paper reports no perception result isolating it — [§3.2](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- **Symmetry and even eyes support honesty judgements:** the authors attribute the baseline's trustworthiness to the possibility that "the symmetrical structure of the face and the large, even eyes" played a role, citing Zebrowitz's *Reading Faces* on features that promote a perception of honesty — [§5.3.3](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- **Baby schema (Lorenz's Kindchenschema) — the experimental evidence:** Glocker et al. (2009) parametrically manipulated photographs of real infant faces and found that **high baby-schema infants were rated as cuter and elicited stronger motivation for caretaking** than unmanipulated and low baby-schema infants; the authors describe it as the first experimental proof of baby-schema effects in actual infant faces — [Glocker et al., *Ethology* 2009 (UPenn PDF)](https://www.cceb.upenn.edu/csa/assets/user-content/documents/BabySchemainInfantFacesInducesCutenessPerceptionandMotivationforCaretakinginAdults.pdf) / [UPenn repository record](https://repository.upenn.edu/entities/publication/2bfa14b1-140d-43bc-94ac-6dbffa99194a).
- Glocker's group also used fMRI to show that baby faces with more baby-schema features generated greater activation in the **nucleus accumbens** (reward circuitry) — reported secondhand via [Wikipedia: Cute](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Cute); not verified against the primary paper.
- **Replication is partial:** a later study using Glocker's own measurements found significant differences in the expected direction only for Caucasian faces, on **face width (p = .018)** and the **ratio of forehead length to face length (p = .014)** — a narrower result than the original — [PLOS ONE (PMC table)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4386811/table/pone.0121554.t004).
- A later paper argues **eye size as an individual feature** can affect cuteness perception independently — [PMC8786738](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8786738).
- Lorenz himself was reportedly sceptical of a generalised cross-species cuteness response, and animal evidence is not uniform — [Discover Magazine](https://www.discovermagazine.com/why-babies-are-so-cute-and-why-we-react-the-way-we-do-40634).
- **Exposure shifts the baseline:** after watching an animated show featuring large-eyed cartoon characters (vs a live-action show), participants showed "a marked increase in preference for human eyes that were larger than normal" — a reason the authors argue cartoon-like robot faces still need careful design under repeated exposure — [Kalegina et al. 2018 §2](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- Kalegina et al. summarise the evolutionary-biology literature as: **infantile features — large eyes, large head, small mouth — evoke a nurturing response in observers** — [§5.3.5](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Concrete rule: large eyes, widely spaced, each with a visible pupil, and a small mouth.** Each of those four clauses has direct empirical backing: small eyes cost likability; close-set eyes cost perceived intelligence; absent pupils cost friendliness/trust/likability and read as soulless; small mouth is part of the documented baby-schema cluster.
- **Concrete rule: put the eyes in the vertical centre or slightly raised, and keep the face symmetrical.** Centred is the modal practice (38.2%); the raised-eyes variant drew no significant penalty on any scale; symmetry and even eyes are the authors' stated candidate explanation for the baseline's trustworthiness.
- **Concrete rule: space eyes roughly midway between the face centre and the face edge** (the 43.9% modal practice), which is the "far" end of real practice and avoids the close-set penalty without becoming an outlier.
- **Pupils before everything.** If the design budget allows exactly one detail inside the eye, it is a pupil. If it allows two, add an iris ring (highest-liked, raises human-likeness, no gender/maturity side effects).
- A catchlight is best understood as *unmeasured but plausibly valuable*: it is the standard animation device for conveying wetness/life, it is tracked as a real design variable in the robot-face census, and it is the cheapest way to add "pupil-like" interior structure. But there is **no HRI study isolating its effect** — treat it as craft knowledge, not evidence.
- Baby schema is the right theoretical frame but should be applied as a *cluster*, not a single dial: the strongest-replicating measurements were face width and forehead-to-face ratio, i.e. a **wide, high-foreheaded face with the eyes low in it**, not merely big eyes.

### Gaps
- **No study gives an optimal eye-diameter-to-face-width ratio.** The 1/20–1/2 screen-area band is descriptive of existing practice, not an optimum. Kalegina's "small eyes" and "large eyes" are categorical, not numeric.
- **No HRI evidence on catchlight/specular highlight** specifically, or on optimal pupil-to-iris size ratio.
- I did not reach the Disney/Pixar animation literature on eye design within budget. The one adjacent empirical source found is the Disney Research/CMU blink paper (§6) — [Modeling and Animating Eye Blinks (Disney Research PDF)](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/Modeling-and-Animating-Eye-Blinks-Paper.pdf). The classic practitioner texts (Thomas & Johnston's *The Illusion of Life*; the "eyes are the window" animation heuristics) were not verified against a source here and should not be cited as research.

## 4. Is a mouth necessary? What eyes alone can and cannot express

### Takeaway
Yes for a *friendly* face, and the evidence is unusually clear. A mouth is not needed for anger, fear, surprise or disgust — eyes alone carry those at full strength — but **happiness specifically collapses without a mouth**, and a mouthless robot face is read as unfriendly, untrustworthy, unlikable, unemotional and surveillance-like.

### Cited Findings — evidence FOR a mouth
- Removing the mouth from the baseline face made it **significantly less friendly (the most unfriendly face of the whole set), significantly less trustworthy, significantly less likable (lowest of the set), significantly less intelligent, and significantly more machine-like.** Participant comments: "the fact that this robot has no mouth makes it seem very unemotional"; "this robot is kind of scary and just seems that it would be just watching as for security purposes" — [Kalegina et al. 2018 §5.3.1–5.3.4, §6](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- 65.6% of the 157 real rendered robot faces have a mouth — it is the majority practice — [§3.3.2](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- **The decisive expression result:** in a between-subjects study with **305 participants**, overall emotion-recognition accuracy from **full face was 64.4%** vs **51.3% from the eye region only** — a significant drop (p < 0.001). Breaking it down: **Fear, Anger, Surprise and Disgust showed no significant difference** between eye-only and full-face; **Happy, Sad and Neutral were recognised significantly worse from the eyes alone, with Happy showing the largest loss.** The authors explicitly identify the lost "mouth cues for happiness" and recommend modelling the whole face where possible — [Pasquali et al. / "Perception of Emotions in Human and Robot Faces: Is the Eye Region Enough?" arXiv:2410.14337](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14337v1) / [MPI record](https://www.mpi.nl/publications/item3610681/perception-emotions-human-and-robot-faces-eye-region-enough).
- Same study, appearance interaction: with the **eye region only**, recognition was significantly lower for the mechanical-looking robot (Titan) than for the human or the humanlike robot (Hayden), which did not differ from each other. **With the full face, those appearance differences disappeared** — [arXiv:2410.14337](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14337v1).
- Robots with mouths, especially in the form of a **smile**, were frequently placed in entertainment and education categories across both Kalegina studies — [§6](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).

### Cited Findings — evidence that a MINIMAL mouth suffices
- Bennett & Šabanović's MiRAE robot used only **two linear feature sets — upper (eye/brow) and lower (mouth)** — with "strict adherence to the minimal features (Action Units) without addition of extraneous attributes, e.g. ears or aesthetic properties," built from feature-selection research showing a small number of FACS Action Units capture ~95% of the information in human facial expressions. It cost **$150–175** and could be built in under a day — [Bennett & Šabanović, HRI 2013 (PDF)](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf).
- MiRAE recognition accuracy (n=30, 7-option forced choice, embodied version): **Happy 96.7%, Sad 100%, Anger 86.7%, Surprise 96.7%, Fear 43.3%.** Including "other" responses: Happy 96.7%, Sad 100%, Anger 93.3%, Surprise 100%, Fear 63.3% — [Bennett & Šabanović 2013, Table I](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf).
- These minimal-face rates **matched or beat far more complex robot faces**: Kismet (n=17) Happy 82%, Sad 82%, Anger 76%, Fear 47%, Surprise 82%, Disgust 71%; Feelix (n=86) Happy 60%, Sad 70%, Anger 40%, Fear 16%, Surprise 37%; BERT (n=10) Happy 99%, Sad 100%, Anger 64%, Fear 44%, Surprise 93%, Disgust 18% — [Bennett & Šabanović 2013, Table II](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf).
- Perceived **strength** of expression correlated strongly with identification accuracy (r² = .896 for the embodied version) — [Bennett & Šabanović 2013 §III](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf).
- An earlier Feelix study found adults recognised emotions in still images of the robot about as well as in human faces — cited in [arXiv:2410.14337](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14337v1).

### Inferences
- **Concrete rule: include a mouth — but it only needs to be a single line/curve.** MiRAE proves a one-stroke lower feature plus a one-stroke upper feature reaches 87–100% recognition on four of five basic emotions, beating much richer faces. So the mouth's cost is one animatable curve, and its measured benefit is friendliness, trust, likability, perceived warmth and *happiness legibility*.
- **Concrete rule: a mouth can be hidden at rest and revealed on expression — but "happy" must show it.** Since Fear/Anger/Surprise/Disgust lose nothing without a mouth, a design could run eyes-only for most states. Happiness, sadness and neutral are the three states where eyes alone measurably degrade — and happiness is the whole point of a friendly home robot.
- **Concrete rule: do not ship a permanently mouthless face.** This is the strongest single negative finding in the literature: across two independent Kalegina studies, no-mouth faces were unfriendly, unlikable, machine-like, and spontaneously assigned by participants to *surveillance*. For a household device that is the worst possible connotation.
- The eye-region study's appearance interaction implies a further rule: **if you do go eyes-only, more humanlike eye geometry is required to stay legible**; a mechanical/abstract eye paired with eyes-only expression is the worst combination.

### Gaps
- Bennett & Šabanović did not run an eyes-only vs mouth-only ablation on their own minimal face, so the relative contribution of their upper vs lower feature set is unmeasured.
- No study found that tests a *mouth that appears only during expression* against an always-visible mouth.

## 5. Eyebrows: how much expressive power do they add?

### Takeaway
Eyebrows are expressively powerful (they are half of the minimal two-stroke FACS face and carry anger in particular) but they come with a measured **cost: they make the face read as significantly more mature/older and more human-like** — both undesirable for a cute household character.

### Cited Findings
- 40.8% of the 157 rendered robot faces have eyebrows — the second-most-common feature after the mouth — [Kalegina et al. 2018 §3.3.2](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- Adding eyebrows made the face **significantly more human-like** and **significantly more mature** than baseline — in fact the eyebrow face was **rated the most mature of all 17 faces**, with participants describing it as "older," "elderly," and "fun to spend time with for adults" — [§5.3.4, §5.3.5](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- Eyebrows did **not** significantly reduce friendliness, trust or likability — they are the one "extra" feature with a clean record on the warmth scales — [§5.3.1, §5.3.7](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- The eyebrow variant was one of the two most popular faces for the **home** context — "the robot face with eyebrows" from Study 2 — [§6](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- The two friendliest *real* robots in Study 1 (Yumi, and one other) **both had short, linear eyebrows**, with participants citing the eyebrows as an attractive, friendly feature — [§6](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- Kalegina also flags a design pitfall: their eyebrow design affected the reading in ways they attribute to specific brow geometry — [§5.3.2 commentary](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- Expressive power: in Bennett & Šabanović's minimal face, the **upper (eye/brow) linear feature set is one of only two feature sets in the entire design**, derived from machine-learning feature selection identifying the critical FACS Action Units; this two-set face achieved 86.7% on **anger** and 96.7% on **surprise** — expressions whose FACS signature is dominated by brow movement (AU4 brow lowerer; AU1/2 brow raiser) — [Bennett & Šabanović 2013](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Concrete rule: use short, straight, thin brows — and consider making them appear only during expression.** Short linear brows are what the empirically friendliest real faces had; the maturity penalty is attached to prominent/angled brow designs. A brow that is absent at rest and appears as a transient stroke for "concerned"/"curious"/"surprised" gets the expressive power without the permanent aging effect.
- **Concrete rule: do not use eyebrows as the primary channel for anger on a friendly home robot.** Lowered brows (AU4) are the strongest anger signal available and will be read as such even in minimal form — which is exactly the reading to avoid. Note Gongzi in Study 1 was the *least* friendly and least trustworthy face and participants described it as having "angry eyes, possibly used for surveillance."
- Eyebrows are the best-evidenced *optional fourth feature* for expressiveness, and irises the best-evidenced optional fourth feature for likability. If both are wanted, brows-on-demand plus a permanent iris resolves the tension.

### Gaps
- **No study found that isolates eyebrow-only expression** (i.e. expression conveyed by brows with eyes and mouth held static). The eyebrow contribution is inferred from FACS Action Unit selection and from the eye-region-only study, which cropped to the *eye region* (brows included) rather than brows alone.

## 6. Blink rate, gaze direction, averted gaze and "thinking" behaviour

### Takeaway
Motion is how a face reads as *alive*, and it partly rescues static-face design sins. The best-evidenced rules: blink at human resting rate with jittered (log-normal) intervals and **fully closing** lids; avert gaze **upward** when "thinking"; and do not assume constant mutual gaze is friendly — it is not.

### Cited Findings
- **Human baseline blink rate:** ~**17 blinks/minute** at rest, rising to ~**26/minute during conversation**, and falling to ~**4.5/minute while reading** — [Make Magazine, "10 Ways to Make Your Robot More Humanlike"](https://makezine.com/2014/05/29/10-ways-to-make-your-robot-more-humanlike/) (secondary source; treat the exact figures as indicative).
- **A robot implementation that worked:** the iCub conversational blinking module used an average of **23.3 blinks/minute**, and human-like blinking made the robot seem **more intelligent** than either no blinking or statistically-timed blinking. The authors also conclude naturalistic blinking alone "is not enough for a robot to meet the requirements for its social integration" — [iCub blinking study (project page)](https://alecive.github.io/research/2016/11/02/iCubBlinking/).
- **Blink timing distribution:** human blink frequency typically follows a **log-normal distribution** — so sampling varied intervals looks more natural than a fixed metronomic rate — reported via the same blink literature ([iCub blinking](https://alecive.github.io/research/2016/11/02/iCubBlinking/)).
- **Blink dynamics (the animation-craft finding with actual data behind it):** Disney Research / CMU found that blinks generated from a **model of real human blink data, with fully closing eyelids**, were consistently perceived as **more natural** than blinks created using the various blink dynamics proposed in animation textbooks — [Modeling and Animating Eye Blinks, Disney Research / CMU (PDF)](https://la.disneyresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/Modeling-and-Animating-Eye-Blinks-Paper.pdf) / [CMU RI record](https://www.ri.cmu.edu/publications/modeling-and-animating-eye-blinks).
- **Gaze aversion as a thinking signal:** Andrist et al. built gaze aversion into a NAO robot using statistics from human conversation. People look away to signal thinking, **usually upward**. Well-timed gaze aversions led to better management of the conversational floor and the robot being perceived as **more thoughtful**. Notably, users did **not** report more comfort with a gaze-averting robot than a static-gaze one — which Andrist described as surprising — and he suggested gaze aversion matters more the more lifelike the robot is.
- **Mutual gaze is not simply good:** Koller et al. (2023), with a Pepper robot and **n=101**, varied the gaze-aversion ratio across its full range and measured feelings of being attended to, comfort, and perceived capabilities; they conclude that **high mutual gaze is not always a sign of high comfort** — [Koller et al. 2023, Frontiers in Robotics and AI](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2023.1062714) / [PDF](https://frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2023.1062714/pdf).
- Mishra et al. (2023, within-subjects, **n=33**) tested whether a robot's gaze aversion changes participants' own gaze aversion — [Does a robot's gaze aversion affect human gaze aversion? (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10326846/). A related child–robot study frames gaze aversion as "an effortless strategy that people commonly use to facilitate thinking when asked challenging questions."
- **Motion compensates for sparse features:** Kalegina et al.'s own stated limitation is that participants saw only static images, and that "a face without pupils may not appear as 'soulless' if it is able to blink," and that Jibo (rated unfriendly as a static image) "could convey their emotive qualities through motion and thus be less reminiscent of a machine" — [§6 Limitations](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf). In Study 1, one robot's low friendliness was offset by what was "signaled by its bodily movements and animated pupil" — [§4.3](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- **Adding body motion dramatically improves expression legibility:** adding **neck motion** to the minimal MiRAE face pushed identification accuracy to **100% for happy, sad, anger and surprise** (from 96.7%, 100%, 86.7%, 96.7%) — and fear to 40% main / 86.7% including "other." Perceived expression strength also rose across the board (e.g. fear 6.25 → 8.60) — [Bennett & Šabanović 2013, Table III](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Concrete rule: blink at ~15–20/min at rest, ~25/min while in conversation, and sample intervals from a log-normal distribution rather than a fixed period.** Backed by the human baseline figures and the iCub implementation's 23.3/min conversational rate.
- **Concrete rule: the lid must fully close during a blink, and the close should be faster than the open.** The Disney/CMU result is that real-data blink dynamics with full closure beat textbook animation curves on perceived naturalness.
- **Concrete rule: for "thinking," avert gaze upward, then return.** Upward is the documented human direction and the one that produced "more thoughtful" ratings.
- **Concrete rule: do not hold constant mutual gaze.** Two independent studies (Andrist; Koller et al. n=101) undercut the assumption that more eye contact means more comfort.
- **Design leverage: a single animated channel buys more legibility than extra static features.** Neck motion alone took a two-stroke face from 87% to 100% on anger. For a minimal face, invest in motion (blink, saccade, gaze shift, a nod/tilt equivalent) before adding a nose, ears or hair.
- **Caveat the static findings accordingly.** The eyelid penalty and (partly) the no-pupil penalty in Kalegina's study are static-image artefacts by the authors' own admission. An eyelid that exists *only* as a blink is a different design object from a drawn, permanently lowered lid.

### Gaps
- **No study found that varies blink rate as an independent variable and measures perceived animacy/aliveness.** The iCub result compares human-like vs statistical vs no blinking, not rate levels, and the sources available did not confirm whether the animacy subscale itself improved.
- The Andrist NAO gaze-aversion study was reached only through secondary summary (IEEE Spectrum coverage) and the primary citation could not be confirmed within budget. The Make Magazine blink figures are also secondary.

## 7. Cultural differences: East Asian vs Western emphasis on eyes vs mouth — and the Indian-household question

### Takeaway
The eyes-vs-mouth cultural split is well established for Japan vs the US/Netherlands: Japanese viewers weight the **eyes**, Westerners weight the **mouth**, and their emoticons differ accordingly (^_^ varies the eyes; :-) varies the mouth). **I found no study placing Indian viewers on this axis** — this is the biggest evidence gap for the stated product context, and the safe design response is to make eyes *and* mouth both carry the signal redundantly.

### Cited Findings
- **The core study:** Yuki, Maddux & Masuda (2007), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 43, 303–311. Japanese participants gave **more weight to the eyes** when judging emotion; Americans gave **more weight to the mouth** — [Yuki, Maddux & Masuda 2007 (author PDF)](https://willmaddux.web.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/15846/2018/04/JESP-Face-Paper.pdf) / [Hokkaido University repository PDF](https://eprints.lib.hokudai.ac.jp/repo/huscap/all/22527/JESP43-2.pdf) / [Kellogg summary](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2007/are-the-windows-to-the-soul-the-same-in).
- **The theoretical mechanism:** the eyes are harder to voluntarily control than the mouth, so the authors predicted that people in cultures that discourage overt emotional display would attend to the eyes — [Yuki et al. 2007 PDF](https://willmaddux.web.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/15846/2018/04/JESP-Face-Paper.pdf); Masuda's framing that eyes are "quite subtle," fitting a culture that masks emotion, is reported in [ScienceDaily coverage](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/04/070404162321.htm) and [LiveScience](https://www.livescience.com/1498-americans-japanese-read-faces-differently.html).
- **The emoticon evidence (directly design-relevant):** Japanese emoticons for happiness and sadness **vary in how the eyes are depicted**, while American emoticons **vary with the direction of the mouth** — [Yuki et al. 2007](https://willmaddux.web.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/15846/2018/04/JESP-Face-Paper.pdf); summarised in [Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-red-light-district/201608/the-cross-cultural-significance-emoticons).
- **Replication beyond the US:** a later Japan-vs-Netherlands study found the same pattern — Japanese participants focusing on eyes, Dutch participants on the mouth — [Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology (SAGE)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022022117734372).
- **The anime extension is speculative:** commentary suggests the pattern may explain why Japanese anime characters have large eyes and small mouths — [Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/us/blog/culture-conscious/201804/why-do-anime-characters-have-huge-eyes-and-tiny-mouths) — but this was **not tested by the study**; treat it as a hypothesis, not a finding.
- **Cross-cultural warning for emoji/emoticon use generally:** [World Economic Forum summary](https://www.weforum.org/stories/all/think-twice-before-you-use-emoticons-people-from-other-countries-might-not-understand-you/).
- **Robot appearance preferences are themselves culture-dependent:** Li, Rau & Li (2010), "A cross-cultural study: Effect of robot appearance and task," *International Journal of Social Robotics* 2(2), 175–186 — cited throughout [Kalegina et al. 2018](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf), which also uses Rau et al.'s humanoid/zoomorphic/mechanical embodiment taxonomy.
- **Kalegina's sample is culturally skewed and the authors flag it:** their 157-robot corpus is 33.8% US-origin, and among their listed future work is to "examine the question of a robot's perceived ethnicity" — [§3.3.1, §6 Limitations](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/assets/pdfs/2018/kalegina2018hri.pdf).
- Bennett & Šabanović planned Japan–US cross-cultural experiments on robotic facial cues for spring 2013 — [Bennett & Šabanović 2013, Conclusion](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf) — indicating the cross-cultural minimal-face question was open as of that paper.

### Inferences
- **Concrete rule: make emotion redundant across eyes and mouth; never let a state be distinguishable by the mouth alone.** This is the direct design consequence of the eyes/mouth cultural split: a face whose only difference between "happy" and "sad" is the mouth curve will be less legible to eye-weighting viewers, and vice versa. Change *both* channels for every state.
- **Concrete rule: give the eyes a shape-change capability, not just a gaze direction.** The Japanese emoticon convention (^_^ vs ;_; vs >_<) is eye-*shape* variation. An eye that can arc, squint, widen and narrow gives you the eye-channel signal that eye-weighting cultures read.
- For an **Indian household** specifically, the honest position is that the Yuki et al. axis has not been measured there. India is neither clearly in the Japanese nor the American cluster on the collectivism/display-rule dimensions the theory invokes, and single-country generalisation from Japan to "East Asian" to "Asian" to "Indian" is an unwarranted chain. Redundant encoding is robust to being wrong about which channel dominates, which makes it the correct engineering choice under this uncertainty.
- A secondary consequence: avoid pink blush/cheeks as a happiness signal. Beyond its measured gender and likability costs (§2), blush is a culturally specific anime/kawaii convention rather than a universally-read emotion cue.

### Gaps
- **No study found on Indian participants' eyes-vs-mouth weighting in facial emotion perception, and none on Indian users' robot-face preferences.** This is a genuine hole in the literature and the single highest-value piece of primary research the project could do (a small eye-vs-mouth forced-choice emoticon study, replicating Yuki et al.'s method with Indian participants, would be cheap).
- No cross-cultural results found from Bennett & Šabanović's planned Japan–US minimal-face study — unclear whether it was published.
- Li, Rau & Li (2010)'s specific cross-cultural findings were not read in primary form; only its citation in Kalegina et al. was verified.

## 8. Expression legibility: which emotions are reliably recognised from a minimal face, and which are confused

### Takeaway
A two-stroke minimal face achieves near-ceiling recognition for **happy, sad, surprise** and good recognition for **anger** — matching or beating complex mechanical faces. **Fear is the universal failure case** (16–47% across every robot face ever measured) and **disgust is unreliable**. Fear→surprise and disgust→anger are the documented confusions.

### Cited Findings
- **Minimal face, embodied (MiRAE, n=30, 7-option forced choice):** Happy **96.7%**, Sad **100%**, Anger **86.7%**, Surprise **96.7%**, Fear **43.3%**. Digital-avatar version: Happy 100%, Sad 100%, Anger 100%, Fear 53.3%, Surprise 86.7% — [Bennett & Šabanović, HRI 2013, Table I](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf).
- **Fear fails on every robot face measured:** MiRAE 43%, Kismet 47%, Feelix 16%, BERT 44%. The authors note "across all faces, similar patterns can be observed (e.g. fear)" — [Bennett & Šabanović 2013, Table II](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf).
- **Disgust is wildly unstable across faces:** BERT 18%, Kismet 71%, MiRAE digital 86.7% — [Bennett & Šabanović 2013, Tables I–II](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf).
- **The documented confusions:** **Fear was often confused with Surprise**, and **Disgust with Anger**; both fear and disgust were hard to recognise from either the full face or the eye region — [arXiv:2410.14337, n=305](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14337v1).
- **Which emotions survive losing the mouth (repeat of §4, stated as a legibility table):** Fear, Anger, Surprise, Disgust — no significant eye-only vs full-face difference. Happy, Sad, Neutral — significantly worse from the eyes alone, **Happy worst affected** — [arXiv:2410.14337](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14337v1).
- **Overall ceiling for robot emotion recognition is not high:** 64.4% full-face across six emotions in a 305-participant study — [arXiv:2410.14337](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14337v1). Minimal cartoon faces in forced-choice tasks do much better than this, likely because of exaggeration and a smaller response set.
- **Exaggeration is the lever:** perceived expression *strength* correlated at r² = .896 with identification accuracy — [Bennett & Šabanović 2013 §III](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf). Adding neck motion raised both strength ratings and accuracy to 100% for happy/sad/anger/surprise — [Table III](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf).
- **Minimalism does not cost legibility:** the explicitly minimalist MiRAE "produced higher, or at least comparable, identification accuracy rates for all expressions" versus Kismet, Eddie, Feelix and BERT, "despite its minimalist design, low cost, and ease/brevity of construction" — [Bennett & Šabanović 2013 §III](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf). The design rationale: feature-selection research shows a small set of FACS Action Units captures ~95% of the information in human facial expressions — [§I–II](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf).
- Note the "other" response option matters: allowing free-choice answers raised MiRAE's anger from 86.7%→93.3% and fear from 43.3%→63.3%, i.e. some apparent failures are label mismatches rather than illegibility — [Table I](https://homes.luddy.indiana.edu/selmas/BennettSabanovic-HRI2013.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Concrete rule: build the expression set from happy, sad, surprise, and a soft anger/annoyance — and do not attempt fear or disgust.** Fear has never exceeded 47% on any measured robot face; disgust ranges 18–87% and is confused with anger. Neither is wanted on a friendly household product anyway.
- **Concrete rule: differentiate surprise and fear structurally, or drop fear.** Since fear→surprise is the dominant confusion, a minimal face effectively has *one* wide-eyed state. Assign it to surprise/curiosity and give it a positive framing.
- **Concrete rule: exaggerate beyond human proportions.** Strength of expression predicts recognition almost perfectly (r² = .896), and a minimal face has no texture or micro-detail to carry subtlety — amplitude is the only legibility lever available.
- **Concrete rule: pair every facial expression with a body/head movement.** Neck motion took the minimal face to 100% on four emotions and raised perceived strength across the board; this is the cheapest accuracy gain documented anywhere in this literature.
- A plausible synthesis for a minimal face: a small, reliably-recognised vocabulary (neutral, happy, very happy, sad/sorry, surprised/curious, thinking/averted, sleeping/idle, listening) each encoded **redundantly in eye shape + mouth curve + a head motion**, with blink and gaze running continuously underneath for aliveness.

### Gaps
- Bennett & Šabanović report no confusion matrix, only accuracy per expression, so the specific misattributions for their minimal face are unknown.
- The arXiv eye-region study's per-emotion eye-only accuracies exist only in a confusion-matrix figure (Figure 3) not readable as text, so exact per-emotion numbers for eye-only recognition could not be extracted.
- No study found that measures legibility of *non-Ekman* states that a home robot actually needs — listening, thinking, confused, acknowledging, sleepy, charging. The Ekman six are what the field measures, and they are a poor fit for the product's real expression vocabulary.
